CPU vs GPU Bottleneck: What It Means and How to Fix It

Learn what actually limits your FPS, how resolution and game type change the answer, and when a CPU or GPU upgrade is really worth it.

Updated October 2026 · SystemFit PC

Short answer: a bottleneck is simply the part of your PC that limits performance first in a particular game and scenario. A GPU being the limit is normal for many games; a CPU limit matters when it prevents you from reaching your target FPS or causes poor frame-time consistency.

There is no single bottleneck percentage that stays true for every game. Resolution, graphics settings, game engine, frame-rate target, memory, drivers and even the scene you're playing can change which component is limiting performance.

Quick diagnosis: if lowering resolution gives you a large FPS increase, the GPU is more likely to be limiting. If FPS barely changes while GPU utilization stays well below full load, investigate the CPU, RAM, frame caps or the game engine.

A bottleneck is the component that reaches its practical performance limit first for a particular workload. In gaming, the CPU and GPU work together to produce each frame, but they do different jobs.

  • CPU: handles game logic, physics, AI, simulation, draw-call preparation and other game-side work.
  • GPU: renders the image, including geometry, lighting, shadows, textures, effects and ray tracing.

If the CPU cannot prepare frames quickly enough, the GPU can end up waiting. If the GPU needs more time to render each frame, the CPU may finish its work and wait for the GPU.

That means every gaming PC is limited by something in a given scenario. The goal is not to eliminate bottlenecks completely. The goal is to have the right component doing the limiting for the performance you actually want.

CPU-limitedThe processor cannot prepare the next frames quickly enough, so the GPU may be underused.
GPU-limitedThe graphics card needs more time to render each frame, so GPU utilization is usually high.

What a CPU bottleneck looks like

A CPU bottleneck is more likely when your GPU is not close to full utilization while your FPS is below the target you want. One or more CPU threads can be heavily loaded even when total CPU usage looks moderate.

CPU limits are especially noticeable in high-FPS gaming, simulation-heavy games, large open worlds and situations with lots of AI or physics.

What a GPU bottleneck looks like

A GPU bottleneck is more likely when the graphics card is consistently near full utilization and changing GPU-heavy settings changes your FPS substantially.

This is often a perfectly normal result. If your GPU is running near 100% and delivering the FPS you want, you usually do not have a problem that needs fixing.

The best way to diagnose a bottleneck is to look at the game you're actually playing rather than relying only on a calculator number.

1. Watch GPU utilization

If the GPU sits around 95–100% during a repeatable gameplay section, it is probably doing most of the limiting work. High GPU utilization is generally desirable when you're getting the performance you want.

2. Look at CPU usage per core

Total CPU utilization can hide a CPU bottleneck. A game may depend heavily on one or two threads while the remaining cores are relatively quiet. Look at per-core or per-thread usage when possible.

3. Lower the resolution

Run the same scene at a lower resolution while keeping the rest of the settings as similar as possible.

  • If FPS rises substantially, the GPU was likely a major limitation.
  • If FPS changes only slightly, the CPU, memory, game engine or another limit may be setting the pace.

4. Watch frame times and 1% lows

Average FPS does not tell the whole story. A system can show a high average while still producing uneven frame times or poor 1% lows. If the game feels stuttery, investigate frame-time consistency rather than chasing a single average-FPS number.

Resolution changes the amount of work the GPU has to perform. As resolution increases, the graphics card generally becomes more important to frame time.

This is why the same CPU and GPU can be CPU-limited at 1080p but GPU-limited at 1440p or 4K. A useful bottleneck estimate therefore needs context rather than treating a hardware pairing as having one permanent answer.

For example, suppose your target is 90 FPS at 1440p and your GPU consistently delivers around 90 FPS while operating close to full load. There is no reason to upgrade the CPU just because the GPU is the limiting component.

A GPU bottleneck is only a problem when the GPU is preventing you from achieving the performance, image quality or frame-rate target you actually care about.

A CPU bottleneck matters when the processor prevents your graphics card from delivering the performance you want, particularly at high frame rates.

For example, a powerful GPU paired with an older CPU may produce a situation where lowering the resolution from 1440p to 1080p barely changes FPS. The GPU has plenty of rendering capability left, but the CPU is not feeding it frames quickly enough.

CPU limitations can also show up as poor 1% lows, inconsistent frame times or slowdowns in busy areas even when average FPS looks acceptable.

Be careful with calculators that tell you a PC has a fixed value such as “17% CPU bottleneck.” That number should be treated as an estimate, not a measured amount of FPS loss.

A bottleneck changes with:

  • Game and game engine
  • Resolution
  • Graphics settings
  • Ray tracing and upscaling
  • Target FPS and monitor refresh rate
  • RAM capacity, speed and configuration
  • Drivers, temperatures and power limits
  • The exact scene being rendered

Some current calculator methodologies explicitly treat bottleneck results as scenario-based estimates because a single universal percentage cannot represent every workload.

Lower CPU-heavy settings

Depending on the game, settings such as crowd density, object distance, simulation quality, NPC count and physics can place more pressure on the processor. Lowering those settings can sometimes improve CPU-limited performance.

Close unnecessary background tasks

Browsers, recording software and other applications can consume CPU resources. Closing unnecessary programs can reduce background contention.

Check temperatures and clocks

A CPU that is overheating or power-limited may not sustain its expected clocks. Check temperatures, clock speeds and throttling before assuming the hardware itself is simply too slow.

Upgrade the CPU when the limitation is consistent

If the same CPU limitation appears across the games you care about and prevents you from reaching your target FPS, a CPU/platform upgrade can make sense.

Lower GPU-heavy settings

Shadows, reflections, volumetric effects, ray tracing and some lighting settings can have a large effect on GPU workload. Reducing them can increase FPS.

Use upscaling

DLSS, FSR, XeSS and similar technologies can reduce the internal rendering workload and reconstruct the final image. The exact quality and performance benefit depends on the game and implementation.

Reduce resolution

Moving from 4K to 1440p, or 1440p to 1080p, can substantially reduce GPU workload when the graphics card is the limiting component.

Upgrade the GPU when it is the real limit

If your GPU is consistently near full load and still cannot deliver the image quality or FPS target you want, upgrading the graphics card is usually more useful than replacing a CPU that is already keeping up.

Do not upgrade just because a calculator says your parts are mismatched. First decide what performance you are trying to achieve.

Upgrade the CPUWhen CPU limits repeatedly hold FPS below your target, especially in high-FPS or CPU-heavy games.
Upgrade the GPUWhen the GPU is consistently the limit and you cannot reach your desired FPS or graphics quality.
Keep your current hardwareWhen your games already meet your FPS and visual-quality targets. A theoretical bottleneck is not automatically a problem.
Investigate firstWhen performance is unexpectedly poor. Check temperatures, clocks, RAM, drivers, power settings and background tasks before buying anything.

Is a 100% GPU bottleneck bad?

No. High GPU utilization is often normal and desirable when the GPU is delivering the FPS you want.

Can a bottleneck change between games?

Yes. Different game engines and workloads can put very different demands on the CPU and GPU.

Can the same PC be CPU-bottlenecked at 1080p and GPU-bottlenecked at 4K?

Yes. Increasing resolution generally increases GPU workload, so the limiting component can change.

Does a stronger GPU always need a stronger CPU?

Not automatically. The answer depends on your resolution, game, settings and FPS target. A powerful GPU can be perfectly reasonable with a mid-range CPU for 1440p or 4K gaming.

Should I trust a bottleneck percentage?

Use it as a rough directional estimate rather than a precise measurement. Real-world monitoring in the game you care about is more useful for an upgrade decision.
