Which Graphics Settings Affect FPS the Most?

A practical guide to the settings that actually reduce GPU load, what you can lower without ruining image quality, and what to change first when a game runs poorly.

Updated October 2026 · SystemFit PC

Quick answer: The settings that usually have the biggest effect on GPU performance are resolution, ray tracing, shadows, volumetrics, reflections and view distance. Texture quality is different: it mainly uses VRAM and does not automatically deliver a large FPS gain when lowered. Start with the settings that are expensive for your hardware instead of turning everything to Low.

Why graphics settings affect FPS differently

A graphics option changes a different part of the rendering workload. Resolution increases the number of pixels the GPU must shade. Ray tracing adds expensive lighting calculations. Shadows, volumetrics and reflections can add substantial work every frame. Other options, such as texture quality, can have a much smaller effect on average FPS when your GPU has enough VRAM.

Graphics settings change GPU workload, but your CPU can also become the limit. See our CPU vs GPU bottleneck guide for how to tell which component is limiting FPS.

That is why a blanket “Low preset” is often a poor troubleshooting method. You can sometimes recover a large amount of performance by lowering two or three expensive settings while leaving many others at High.

A better order for tuning a game

  1. Check your resolution and render scale. 4K can be dramatically harder to render than 1440p or 1080p.
  2. Test ray tracing. If RT is enabled, compare the game with RT off before sacrificing normal rasterization quality.
  3. Reduce shadows, volumetrics and reflections. These are common sources of expensive GPU work.
  4. Adjust view distance or LOD. If GPU usage is not high, this may not solve the real problem and can sometimes shift work toward the CPU.
  5. Choose an upscaler. DLSS, FSR or XeSS can reduce the internal rendering resolution while reconstructing the final image.
  6. Only then chase small settings. Anti-aliasing, effects and ambient-occlusion changes may produce smaller gains.

Textures are different

Texture quality primarily determines how detailed surface textures look and how much VRAM the game needs. If your GPU has enough VRAM, lowering textures from Ultra to Medium may barely change average FPS while making the game look noticeably worse.

If you are running out of VRAM, however, texture quality can become important. You may see texture pop-in, hitching, degraded texture quality or other streaming problems. In that situation, reducing textures can help stabilize the experience even when the average-FPS number barely changes.

Rule of thumb: If you have plenty of VRAM, protect texture quality. If VRAM is near its limit, textures become a much more useful setting to adjust.

What if lowering settings barely changes FPS?

If you lower several GPU-heavy settings and FPS barely moves, the GPU may not be the limiting component. Check GPU utilization, CPU utilization, temperatures, clock speeds and RAM usage while the problem is happening.

A CPU-limited game can remain at roughly the same FPS even after the graphics quality is reduced. A frame-rate cap, background process, thermal throttling or game-engine limitation can also make a graphics change appear ineffective.

Graphics quality vs FPS: practical presets

You want maximum FPSDisable RT, use a sensible upscaler, reduce shadows/volumetrics/reflections, and keep textures high if VRAM allows.
You want a balanced imageKeep textures High, use Medium/High shadows and volumetrics, and use Quality or Balanced upscaling when available.
You want maximum visual qualityUse native or high-quality upscaling, then enable RT only if your GPU has enough performance headroom.
You have a CPU limitLowering GPU settings may not help much. Look at CPU load, game settings such as view distance and background processes.
