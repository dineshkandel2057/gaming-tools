SSD vs HDD for Gaming: What Actually Changes?

Understand loading times, asset streaming, NVMe vs SATA SSDs, DirectStorage and what storage does—and does not—do to gaming performance.

Updated October 2026 · SystemFit PC

Quick answer: Moving a game from an HDD to an SSD can dramatically improve loading times and storage responsiveness, but it does not normally turn 60 FPS into 90 FPS. In a new gaming PC, an SSD should be the primary game drive; an HDD can still make sense for inexpensive bulk storage.

Does an SSD increase FPS?

Usually, not meaningfully. Once the game has the data it needs for rendering, the CPU, GPU, VRAM and RAM do most of the work involved in producing frames. Storage becomes more important when the game is launching, loading a level, saving data or streaming new assets.

That distinction matters because storage upgrades are sometimes advertised as FPS upgrades. An SSD can make a PC feel dramatically faster without being a major average-FPS upgrade.

SATA SSD vs NVMe

Both are solid-state storage, but NVMe drives communicate over PCIe and can provide much higher sequential bandwidth and lower overhead than SATA SSDs. In gaming, however, benchmark bandwidth does not translate directly into proportional FPS.

A good SATA SSD is already a huge improvement over an HDD. For a new PC, NVMe is often the practical default because M.2 slots are common and fast drives are widely available. You do not need the fastest drive on the market simply because its sequential specification is larger.

Don't overspend for FPS: HDD → SSD is a major quality-of-life upgrade. Good SSD → much faster SSD is usually a much smaller gaming-performance change.

What about DirectStorage?

Microsoft DirectStorage is designed to improve the path used to load game data from storage, with modern requirements centered on NVMe storage and supported DirectX 12 hardware. Support and benefits still depend on the game.

The practical takeaway is simple: modern games increasingly benefit from SSD-class storage, but DirectStorage does not mean every game will suddenly gain a large FPS boost from a faster SSD.

How much storage should a gaming PC have?

Game installs have become large enough that capacity matters almost as much as speed. For many users, 1TB is a sensible starting point, while 2TB gives much more room for several large games, recordings and applications. Your own library matters more than any universal number.

Try to keep reasonable free space on the system/game drive instead of filling it completely. If you record gameplay, store large video files or keep a large game library, a second drive can be more useful than buying an extremely high-end primary SSD.

When an HDD still makes sense

HDDs remain useful when low cost per terabyte matters more than speed. They can be reasonable for archived recordings, backups or files that do not need fast random access.

For games you actively play, especially modern large open-world titles, an SSD is the better default. If a particular game officially requires or recommends SSD storage, follow that game's requirement rather than assuming an HDD will behave the same.

SSD upgrade decision

You still use an HDD for gamesMove frequently played games to an SSD first. The improvement in loading and responsiveness can be substantial.
You already have a SATA SSDKeep it unless capacity or a specific workload is the problem. You do not need NVMe solely to gain FPS.
You are building a new gaming PCChoose a reputable NVMe SSD with enough capacity rather than chasing the highest benchmark number.
You want higher FPSMeasure the CPU/GPU limitation first. Storage is rarely the correct upgrade for a straightforward rendering bottleneck.
